## From Symbolic AI to Deep Learning

$$
z_i = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} v_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}
$$

The story of artificial intelligence begins in the 1950s with **Symbolic AI**, also known as "Good Old-Fashioned AI" (GOFAI). The core idea was simple: intelligence could be captured by manipulating symbols according to explicit, hand-written logical rules. This approach gave rise to **expert systems** in the 1970s and 80s — programs like MYCIN (medical diagnosis) and DENDRAL (chemistry) that encoded the knowledge of human experts as large sets of if-then rules.

Symbolic AI worked well in narrow, well-defined domains but struggled badly with ambiguity, noisy real-world data, and tasks humans find intuitive (recognizing a face, understanding a sentence). This limitation — sometimes called the **knowledge acquisition bottleneck** — pushed the field toward a different philosophy: instead of hand-coding rules, let the system *learn* patterns directly from data.

![From rule-based systems to learning-based systems](https://placehold.co/900x420?text=Symbolic+AI+%E2%86%92+Machine+Learning+%E2%86%92+Deep+Learning)

This shift gave rise to **Machine Learning (ML)**, where statistical algorithms (decision trees, SVMs, logistic regression) learn from labeled examples rather than explicit rules. **Deep Learning (DL)**, a subfield of ML built on multi-layer neural networks, later removed much of the manual feature-engineering step entirely — the network learns its own internal representations directly from raw data (pixels, characters, audio waveforms).

| Era | Approach | Key idea | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1950s–80s | Symbolic AI / Expert Systems | Hand-written logical rules | Doesn't scale, brittle with ambiguity |
| 1990s–2000s | Classical Machine Learning | Learn statistical patterns from labeled data | Needs manual feature engineering |
| 2010s–now | Deep Learning | Learn hierarchical representations automatically | Needs large data & compute |

## The Emergence of Foundation Models

For most of ML history, a model was trained from scratch for one specific task: a spam classifier, a translation model, an image labeler — each isolated, each requiring its own labeled dataset. Around 2018–2020 this changed with the rise of **Foundation Models**: very large models (billions of parameters) pre-trained once on massive, broad, largely unlabeled data, which can then be *adapted* to many downstream tasks with little extra effort.

![Traditional single-task models vs. one adaptable foundation model](https://placehold.co/900x420?text=Traditional+Models+vs+Foundation+Model)

Key differences from traditional task-specific models:

- **Traditional models**: one model → one task, trained from scratch, needs large labeled datasets for *each* task.
- **Foundation models**: one large pre-trained model → many tasks, adapted via fine-tuning, prompting, or few-shot examples, dramatically reducing the labeled-data requirement per task.
- Foundation models exhibit **emergent capabilities** — abilities (like basic arithmetic or multi-step reasoning) that appear only once the model and its training data cross a certain scale, and were not explicitly trained for.

Examples include GPT (text), CLIP (vision-language), Whisper (speech), and Stable Diffusion (image generation) — all built on the same underlying idea of broad pre-training followed by flexible adaptation.

## Generative AI vs. Traditional AI

Most "traditional" AI systems are **discriminative**: given an input, they predict a label, a score, or a category — is this email spam? What object is in this image? **Generative AI** flips the problem: given some input (or nothing at all), it *produces* new content — text, images, audio, code — that did not exist before, by modeling the underlying probability distribution of the data.

![Discriminative AI (classify) vs. Generative AI (create)](https://placehold.co/900x420?text=Discriminative+AI+vs+Generative+AI)

| Aspect | Traditional (Discriminative) AI | Generative AI |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Classify / predict / score | Create new content |
| Typical output | A label or number | Text, image, audio, video, code |
| Example task | Spam detection, fraud scoring | Writing an essay, generating an image |
| Underlying model | Learns a decision boundary | Learns the data distribution itself |

## Operational Applications of Generative AI

Generative AI has moved from research demos to everyday tools across nearly every content type:

- **Text generation**: chatbots, writing assistants, summarization, translation (e.g. ChatGPT, Claude)
- **Image generation**: text-to-image synthesis, editing, style transfer (e.g. Midjourney, Stable Diffusion)
- **Code generation**: autocomplete, full function generation, bug fixing (e.g. GitHub Copilot)
- **Video generation**: text-to-video, video editing and extension (e.g. Sora, Runway)
- **Audio generation**: text-to-speech, music composition, voice cloning
- **Multimodal systems**: models that jointly understand and generate across text, image, and audio in a single conversation (e.g. GPT-4o, Gemini)

![Generative AI across text, image, code, video, and audio](https://placehold.co/900x420?text=Text+%C2%B7+Image+%C2%B7+Code+%C2%B7+Video+%C2%B7+Audio)

The common thread across all of these is a model that has learned a rich, general representation of its domain and can sample new, coherent outputs from it — often conditioned on a natural-language instruction (a **prompt**).

## Introduction to Language Models

A **Language Model (LM)** is, at its core, a system trained to estimate the probability of a sequence of words — or more precisely, to predict the next token given the tokens that came before it:

```
P(w_1, w_2, ..., w_n) = P(w_1) · P(w_2 | w_1) · P(w_3 | w_1, w_2) · ...
```

Early language models were simple **n-gram** models, counting how often word sequences occurred in a corpus. These were later replaced by neural approaches — RNNs and LSTMs — which could capture longer dependencies, and eventually by the **Transformer** architecture (covered in detail in Sessions 4 and 5), which made it possible to train models with billions of parameters on massive text corpora, giving rise to today's **Large Language Models (LLMs)**.

> This is the foundation for everything that follows in the course: tokenization, embeddings, attention, and eventually full LLM-based agentic systems.
