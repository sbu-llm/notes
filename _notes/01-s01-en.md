## <span class="heading-blue">From Symbolic AI to Deep Learning</span>

<img src="/picture/S01/1.png" alt="Descriptive text" width="600">

The story of artificial intelligence begins in the 1950s with **Symbolic AI** – an approach sometimes referred to as "Good Old-Fashioned AI" (GOFAI). The core idea was elegantly simple and compelling: intelligence could be recreated by manipulating symbols according to explicit, hand-coded logical rules. In other words, if we could encode human knowledge and reasoning rules as a set of if-then statements, machines could think and make decisions like humans. This approach rested on the assumption that human intelligence could be reduced to a set of logical rules, and that mental processes could be simulated through symbols and operations performed on them.

This same approach led to **expert systems** in the 1970s and 1980s. These were programs that encoded the knowledge of human experts in the form of vast collections of if-then rules. Among the most famous of these systems were **MYCIN**, designed for diagnosing infectious diseases and recommending antibiotics, and **DENDRAL**, used in chemistry for identifying the molecular structure of organic compounds. These systems performed admirably in limited, well-defined domains and, in some cases, even outperformed human experts. For instance, MYCIN demonstrated superior performance in diagnosing certain blood diseases compared to non-specialist physicians and proved to be a valuable assistive tool in clinical settings.

Despite these successes, Symbolic AI faced fundamental challenges. It struggled severely with ambiguity, noisy real-world data, and tasks that come naturally to humans (such as facial recognition or understanding a sentence). Writing rules for every possible real-world scenario was practically impossible, and even minor changes in conditions could cause the entire system to fail. This limitation, sometimes called the **knowledge acquisition bottleneck**, paved the way for a different philosophy: instead of manually writing rules, let the system **learn** patterns directly from data.

This paradigm shift led to the emergence of **Machine Learning (ML)**. In this new approach, instead of explicit hand-coded rules, statistical algorithms such as decision trees, Support Vector Machines (SVMs), and logistic regression learn from labeled examples. By observing numerous examples, the system identifies hidden patterns in the data and becomes capable of generalizing to new cases. This method offered far greater flexibility than the symbolic approach and could adapt to new data. A landmark achievement of this era was the MNIST handwritten digit recognition competition, where machine learning algorithms achieved high accuracy in recognizing handwritten digits, demonstrating the power of the data-driven approach.

**Deep Learning (DL)**, a subfield of machine learning based on multi-layer neural networks, brought about a fundamental transformation in this domain. This approach largely eliminated the manual feature engineering stage. In deep learning, the network learns its own internal representations directly from raw data (image pixels, text characters, or sound waves), removing the need for human-crafted feature extraction. In other words, the deep neural network autonomously determines which features are important for solving the problem and discovers them across its various layers. This capability, combined with increased computational power and access to massive datasets, enabled deep learning to achieve remarkable breakthroughs in areas such as computer vision, natural language processing, and speech recognition. For instance, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) demonstrated performance exceeding human capability on many standard image recognition datasets.

  <div class="callout-title">

  </div>
  The key takeaway from this evolution is the shift from approaches based on hand-coded rules to those based on learning from data. The more data and the larger the model, the better the system's performance. This simple yet powerful principle underlies all recent advances in artificial intelligence.
</div>

| Period | Approach | Key Idea | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1950s–1980s | Symbolic AI / Expert Systems | Hand-coded logical rules | Not scalable, brittle in face of ambiguity |
| 1990s–2000s | Classical Machine Learning | Learning statistical patterns from labeled data | Required manual feature engineering |
| 2010s–present | Deep Learning | Automatic learning of hierarchical representations | Requires large amounts of data and computation |

## <span class="heading-purple">The Emergence of Foundation Models</span>

<img src="/picture/S01/2.png" alt="Descriptive text" width="600">

Throughout most of machine learning history, each model was trained from scratch for a specific task. A spam classifier, a translation model, an image labeler, a speech recognition system, a sentiment analysis model – each developed separately, each requiring its own labeled dataset. This task-specific approach, while effective in limited domains, faced challenges such as the need for abundant labeled data, high training time and costs, and the inability to transfer knowledge across different tasks. For example, a model trained for facial recognition could not be used for object detection or sentiment analysis; each new application required a completely new model to be designed and trained from the ground up.

Around 2018 to 2020, this landscape was completely transformed by the emergence of **Foundation Models**. Foundation models are extremely large models (with billions of parameters) that are pre-trained once on massive amounts of broad, predominantly unlabeled data. This data is typically collected from the internet, books, scientific articles, and other textual and visual sources, amounting to billions of words or images. After this pre-training phase, these models can be **adapted** with relatively little effort for numerous downstream tasks. This new approach revolutionized how AI systems are developed and deployed, enabling the use of a single general-purpose model across a wide range of applications instead of building specialized models for each use case.

Key differences between foundation models and traditional task-specific models include:

- **Traditional Models**: One model per specific task, trained from scratch for each task, requiring a large labeled dataset for **each** task independently. This approach is time-consuming, costly, and non-scalable; any change in the application domain requires a lengthy and expensive process of data collection and retraining.

- **Foundation Models**: One large pre-trained model that can be used for multiple different tasks. Adaptation is achieved through fine-tuning with limited data, prompt engineering, or few-shot examples. This approach dramatically reduces the need for labeled data for each new task and makes the development process significantly faster and more cost-effective.

Foundation models exhibit **emergent capabilities** – abilities (such as simple arithmetic, multi-step reasoning, understanding abstract concepts, solving math problems, or even generating code) that appear only when the model and its training data reach a certain scale, without having been explicitly trained for them. In other words, these capabilities emerge suddenly and unexpectedly in very large models, indicating a deeper understanding of linguistic and conceptual patterns. This phenomenon is one of the most fascinating aspects of large language models and demonstrates that with increasing scale, models achieve understanding beyond what they were directly trained for.

Prominent examples of these models include: **GPT** (text generation), **CLIP** (joint image-language understanding), **Whisper** (speech recognition and translation), **Stable Diffusion** (text-to-image generation), and **BERT** (semantic language understanding). All these models share the common principle of broad pre-training combined with flexible adaptability, demonstrating the power of the foundation model approach. Each of these models has revolutionized its respective domain and established new performance standards in AI systems.

  <div class="callout-title">
    <span class="callout-icon">ℹ️</span> Info
  </div>
  Foundation models like GPT and CLIP have demonstrated that increasing scale (more data, more parameters, more computation) leads to significant performance improvements and the emergence of new capabilities not present in smaller models. This observation has led to the formulation of "scaling laws" in deep learning, suggesting that exponential increases in data and computation yield linear improvements in performance.
</div>

  What Are Emergent Capabilities? Emergent capabilities are abilities that do not exist in smaller models but suddenly appear once a certain threshold of model size and data volume is crossed. For example, large language models can solve simple math problems, engage in multi-step reasoning, or even write computer code, while smaller models lack these abilities. This phenomenon reflects the complexity and depth of understanding that large models achieve through learning data patterns.
</div>

## <span class="heading-red">Generative AI vs. Traditional AI</span>

<img src="/picture/S01/3.png" alt="Descriptive text" width="600">

Most "traditional" AI systems are **discriminative**. These systems take an input and predict a label, score, or category. For example: Is this email spam? What is present in this image? Is this customer likely dissatisfied with our services? These systems learn decision boundaries and classify data into different categories. Discriminative models are typically trained using labeled data, aiming to find boundaries that separate data from different classes. This approach has been highly successful in classification and prediction problems and continues to be widely used in many industrial applications.

**Generative AI** reverses the problem. These systems take an input (or even no input) and create new content – text, images, audio, code – that did not previously exist. This is achieved by modeling the underlying probability distribution of the data. In other words, generative AI understands the structure and patterns of data and then uses that understanding to produce new, similar samples. Generative models are typically trained using unlabeled or weakly supervised data, and their goal is to learn the data distribution sufficiently well to generate new samples from that same distribution. This ability to create new content has opened new horizons in creative and productive domains.

The fundamental difference between these two approaches lies in their ultimate goals: traditional AI aims for classification and recognition, while generative AI aims for creation and generation of new content. This philosophical difference has led to a wide range of diverse applications for each. While traditional AI finds use in areas like medical diagnosis, security, financial data analysis, and recommendation systems, generative AI excels in content creation, design, art, writing, code generation, and many other creative and productive fields.

|---|---|---|

| Underlying Model | Learning decision boundaries | Learning the data distribution itself |
| Training Data Type | Predominantly labeled | Predominantly unlabeled |
| Primary Application | Analysis, recognition, prediction | Generation, creativity, design |

## <span class="heading-green">Operational Applications of Generative AI</span>

<img src="/picture/S01/4.png" alt="Descriptive text" width="600">

In recent years, generative AI has progressed from research demonstrations to everyday tools across virtually all types of content. This technology is rapidly entering various industries and transforming how work is done. According to published reports, the generative AI market is projected to exceed one trillion dollars by 2030, with profound impacts on the global economy and labor market structure. Below, we explore some of the most significant operational applications of generative AI:

<span class="text-blue">**Text Generation**</span>: Intelligent chatbots that can answer user questions and conduct natural conversations, writing assistants that help authors produce content, automatic summarization systems that condense long texts into concise summaries, advanced machine translation with near-human quality, and marketing content generation that can automatically produce advertising copy and sales brochures. Famous examples include ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and LLaMA, each offering unique capabilities in text generation.

<span class="text-orange">**Image Generation**</span>: Text-to-image conversion that creates photorealistic or artistic images based on textual descriptions, intelligent image editing that enables modification of image elements through text commands, style transfer that applies the style of one artwork to another, restoration of old and damaged photographs, and generation of photorealistic images from text descriptions for use in advertising, product design, and digital arts. Tools like Midjourney, Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 2, and DALL-E 3 operate in this domain, each revolutionizing visual content production.

<span class="text-purple">**Code Generation**</span>: Auto-completion that predicts and suggests subsequent code based on coding patterns, full function generation based on natural language descriptions, bug detection and fixing with intelligent correction suggestions, automatic documentation generation that analyzes code and produces technical documentation, and code conversion between different programming languages that facilitates software migration. GitHub Copilot is one of the most famous tools in this domain, used by millions of developers worldwide, significantly boosting coding productivity.

<span class="text-pink">**Video Generation**</span>: Text-to-video conversion that produces short videos based on textual scenarios, intelligent video editing that enables automatic correction and enhancement, special effects addition through simple commands, complex animation generation, and expansion and extension of existing videos with new content. Services like Sora (from OpenAI), Runway Gen-2, and Pika are leading in this field, producing remarkably high-quality videos that are increasingly difficult to distinguish from real footage.

<span class="text-green">**Audio Generation**</span>: High-quality, natural text-to-speech that can produce voices resembling human speech, automatic music composition that creates new melodies and pieces, voice cloning that can replicate any person's voice with just a few minutes of audio samples, automated podcast production, and sound effect generation for films and video games. Tools like ElevenLabs, Murf.ai, and Suno AI are active in this space.

<span class="text-red">**Multimodal Systems**</span>: Models that simultaneously understand and generate multiple data types (text, image, audio, video) within a single conversation. These models can accept multimodal inputs (for example, an image accompanied by a text question about it) and produce multimodal outputs (for example, describing the image along with audio). This capability makes them increasingly similar to human abilities in understanding and interacting with the world. Notable examples include GPT-4o, Gemini, and Claude 3, which can interact with various data types through a unified interface.

The common thread across all these applications is a model that has learned a rich and general representation of its domain and can sample new, coherent outputs from that representation. This is typically achieved by conditioning on a natural language instruction – a **prompt** – that communicates the user's request to the model. Prompt engineering has become an art form that requires understanding model behavior and the ability to design effective instructions. A well-crafted prompt can make the difference between mediocre and exceptional outputs.

<div class="box-green">
  **Key Point:** Prompt engineering, the art of designing input instructions for large language models, can dramatically improve output quality and generate more precise results. Techniques such as few-shot prompting, Chain-of-Thought, and Self-Consistency are among the advanced methods in this field that will be explored in detail in future sessions.
</div>

## <span class="heading-orange">Introduction to Language Models</span>

At its core, a **Language Model** is a system trained to estimate the probability of a sequence of words. More precisely, these models are designed to predict the next token given the preceding tokens. This is accomplished using the chain rule of probability:

```
P(w_1, w_2, ..., w_n) = P(w_1) · P(w_2 | w_1) · P(w_3 | w_1, w_2) · ...
```

In other words, a language model observes previous words and predicts the probability distribution of the next word. This predictive ability forms the foundation for all applications of language models, including text generation, translation, summarization, question answering, code completion, and many other tasks. In fact, any task involving natural language can be framed as some form of next-token prediction, which is precisely what makes large language models such powerful and versatile tools.

Early language models were simple **n-gram** models that counted how many times each sequence of words appeared in a large text corpus and estimated the probability of new sequences accordingly. While simple and interpretable, these models performed poorly when faced with unseen sequences and could not effectively capture long-range dependencies between words. Additionally, the data sparsity problem caused many valid sequences to receive zero probability, preventing the model from functioning properly.

These models were later replaced by neural approaches. **RNN** (Recurrent Neural Networks) and **LSTM** (Long Short-Term Memory) networks could capture longer dependencies between words and demonstrated significantly better performance. These architectures, using recurrent structures and internal memory, could retain information about previous words and use it to predict subsequent ones. However, these architectures also faced limitations such as the vanishing gradient problem and the inability to process data in parallel, which made training on very large datasets challenging.

Finally, the **Transformer** architecture revolutionized the field of language models. This architecture, which will be examined in detail in sessions 4 and 5, introduced the **Attention** mechanism that enables efficient parallel processing and long-range dependency modeling. In the attention mechanism, the model looks at all input tokens simultaneously and determines the relative importance of each token for predicting the next token. This capability, combined with the Transformer's parallel architecture, made it possible to train models with billions of parameters on massive text corpora, leading to the emergence of today's **Large Language Models (LLMs)**. The Transformer also uses Self-Attention to understand relationships between words within a sentence and Cross-Attention for connecting two different sequences, features that make it an exceptionally powerful architecture.
